Stuck Moments

What to Do When You Do Not Know What to Say in Therapy

A blank moment does not automatically mean you are unprepared. It may mean you need to slow down long enough to identify the clinical task in front of you.

Written by Tiffany McGilloway, LPC · Published October 14, 2026 · Educational content for mental health professionals

Your client finishes speaking and you do not immediately know what should happen next. You may feel pressure to fill the silence, ask a better question, or produce an intervention quickly enough to appear competent.

That pressure can pull your attention away from the client and toward your own performance. A more useful response begins by returning to the room.

Pause before searching for the perfect sentence

A brief pause gives you time to notice the client’s affect, pace, body language, and response to what was just said. It also gives the client time to continue. Silence is not always a problem that requires immediate correction.

Clarify what the moment may need

Ask yourself which task is most relevant right now:

  • Does the client need more room to feel or reflect?
  • Would validation help them feel understood?
  • Is there something unclear that needs gentle exploration?
  • Would summarizing help organize several concerns?
  • Is the process between you worth naming?
  • Has the session lost its focus or direction?

If the conversation repeatedly loses direction, a flexible therapy session structure can help you and the client choose a collaborative focus.

Choose a simple response you can stand behind

Your next move does not have to be impressive. Depending on the case and your clinical judgment, it may be useful to reflect what you heard, ask what the client noticed, summarize the tension, name the silence, or collaboratively revisit the focus.

The goal is not to remove every uncertain moment. It is to keep your attention available so you can respond to the client instead of performing certainty.

Notice what happens next

A response gives you new information. Does the client elaborate, withdraw, correct you, become more emotional, or shift topics? Clinical reasoning continues after you speak. You can adapt rather than treating the first response as a final test of competence.

Review stuck moments specifically

Afterward, document the sequence: what happened before you felt stuck, what you noticed, what you assumed, what you chose, and how the client responded. Bring recurring patterns to supervision or consultation. This turns a vague sense of failure into material for deliberate learning.

This article is general professional education. Responses must be adapted to the client, setting, scope of practice, treatment plan, safety needs, and applicable supervision or consultation.
